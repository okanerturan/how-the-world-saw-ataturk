# Soldiers on Mustafa Kemal in Their Own Words, 1915–1940

Round 16 looked for soldiers who fought with or against Mustafa Kemal, or studied him later, in the open scans of their
memoirs on archive.org (the full-text search found the books; the open text was then read for the exact passage and page).
Six records. They are short, and all six are respectful: no soldier in the open texts calls him anything but capable.

## The pattern

- **The commander above him:** Otto Liman von Sanders (1927) trusted the 19th Division's commander with the Anafarta group
  ('I had full confidence in his energy') and in October 1918 handed over the whole Army Group to him. [[Hans Kannengiesser]]
  (1927) was alongside him on the peninsula and remembered a quiet, self-reliant officer who 'weighed and decided everything for himself'.
- **The opposing army, 1921-22:** [[Prince Andrew of Greece]] (1930) writes of the Greek advance that the Turks owed their survival
  to commanders of 'great military capacity', Kemal and Ismet; [[Charles Harington]] (1940), the Allied commander at Constantinople,
  calls him 'a real soldier' who held the Greeks on the Sakarya and 'saw his chance' at Afyon, but also says that arms the Allies
  had stored fed his army. This is the only conflicted voice of the six.
- **The historians of the war:** B.H. Liddell Hart (1930) puts Kemal with Liman von Sanders among the 'able commanders' Allenby
  had to outwit; [[Archibald Wavell]] (1940), Allenby's biographer, names him among 'two of the best Turkish commanders' of 1918 and
  the future Atatürk 'who was to regenerate the Turkish nation'.


## Otto Liman von Sanders (1927)

*Gives the 19th Division commander the Anafarta group in August 1915, trusting his energy, and in October 1918 hands over the Army Group to Kemal.*

> I had full confidence in his energy.

Five Years in Turkey (English translation, US Naval Institute, 1927), p. 86 (running head '86 FIVE YEARS IN TURKEY'); open DLI scan in.ernet.dli.2015.24341. Same passage, p. 85: 'Mustapha Kemal, who earned his first military laurels in Cyrenaica, was a leader that delighted in responsibility' (OCR reads 'reponsibility'). His farewell order of 31 Oct 1918 (p. 320) hands over to 'H. E. General Mustapha Kemal' 'proved in many a glorious battle'. The German original is Fünf Jahre Türkei (1920). In the same book Liman says Kemal 'did not want' the Seventh Army command in 1918 and 'did not keep it'. He writes as Kemal's commander and later a defeated ally; no later verdict on the president. Location: Liman von Sanders, Five Years in Turkey (1927), pp. 85-86, 320. Evidence P. See Otto Liman von Sanders.


## Hans Kannengiesser (1927)

*Recalls Kemal at Gallipoli as a clear-thinking, quiet, self-reliant commander who knew what he wanted; now 'world-famous President of Turkey'.*

> The impression that I gained of him during this period was that of a clear-thinking, active, quiet man who knew what he wanted.

The Campaign in Gallipoli (Hutchinson, English edition 1928; German Gallipoli, der Wendepunkt des Weltkrieges, 1927), p. 126 (running head '126 THE CAMPAIGN IN GALLIPOLI'); open scan campaigngallipolikannengiesser. The same passage: 'Mustapha Kemal Bey is the now world-famous President of Turkey'; 'He weighed and decided everything for himself, without looking elsewhere for support or agreement'; 'He did not appear to be very strong bodily, although extremely wiry'. A plate in the book is captioned 'Mustapha Kemal Bey, now Pasha and President of the Turkish Republic'. On p. 222 Kannengiesser records the hanging of Djavid Bey 'for rebelling against the government of Mustapha Kemal Pasha' (a fact, no judgement). He had come to take over the 5th Division and agreed that Kemal should conduct the action over the whole front. Neutral voice kept. Location: Kannengiesser, The Campaign in Gallipoli (1928), p. 126. Evidence P. See Gallipoli Campaign.


## Prince Andrew of Greece (1930)

*Says the Greek advance of 1921 might have destroyed the Turkish army but for commanders of 'great military capacity', Kemal and Ismet.*

> if it had not been commanded by leaders of great military capacity as Mustapha Kemal Pasha and Ismet Pasha.

Towards Disaster: The Greek Army in Asia Minor in 1921 (John Murray, 1930), p. 33 (running head 'A NATURALLY STRONG LINE 33'); open scan towardsdisastergreekarmy1921. The quote ends the sentence; the complete clause is 'might almost have caused the destruction of the Turkish Army, if it had not been commanded by leaders of great military capacity as Mustapha Kemal Pasha and Ismet Pasha'. On p. 173 he reports that Turkish officers said Kemal had been 'in the lines' two days earlier 'encouraging and urging his men to a last desperate fight' (a report, no judgement). The book argues the Greek disaster was caused by the Greek command's own decisions, not by the enemy; the voice here is that of a defeated officer on his opponents. Used with neutral voice; Sakarya and Asia Minor are sensitive ground. Location: Prince Andrew of Greece, Towards Disaster (1930), p. 33 (also p. 173). Evidence P. See Greco-Turkish War.


## B. H. Liddell Hart (1930)

*Counts Kemal, with Liman von Sanders, among the 'able commanders' Allenby had to outwit in 1918; at Anzac, the 'then unknown' officer.*

> in 1918 Allenby had to outwit such able commanders as Liman von Sanders and Mustapha Kemal

The Real War 1914-1918 (Faber, 1930), p. 468 (footer '467' precedes the head 'ANNIHILATION OF THE TURKISH ARMIES'; the book's index gives Megiddo 470 ff.); open DLI scan in.ernet.dli.2015.57906. In the Gallipoli chapter, p. 184: 'the initiative of one opponent, the then unknown Mustapha Kemal, contributed to its unfulfilment' (of the Anzac landing's opportunity). A short remark inside a military history: the record is intensity 1. The existing person note on Liddell Hart already carries a general characterisation without a cited page; this record is the sourced one. Location: Liddell Hart, The Real War (1930), pp. 184, 468. Evidence P. See B.H. Liddell Hart.


## Archibald Wavell (1940)

*Calls Kemal one of 'two of the best Turkish commanders' of 1918 and a leader who 'was later to rally and reorganize all Turkey'.*

> the future Ataturk, who was to regenerate the Turkish nation

Allenby: Soldier and Statesman (Harrap, 1946 edition of the 1940 Allenby: A Study in Greatness), p. 236 (index: 'Kemal, Mustapha, Ataturk, 171, 236 and n. 1, 245'); open DLI scan in.ernet.dli.2015.505341. Also p. 245: 'two of the best Turkish commanders, Mustapha Kemal, of the Seventh Army, and Jemal Pasha, of the Fourth'; Aleppo 'rallied and reorganized by no less a leader than Mustapha Kemal Pasha, who was later to rally and reorganize all Turkey'. P. 171: Kemal 'declined to serve under a German staff'. Pagination is the 1946 edition; the 1941 edition (allenbystudyingr0000arch, search-inside) has the same sentences. In Allenby in Egypt (1943), Wavell says Zaghlul 'may have dreamed of a dictatorship like that of Mustapha Kemal' and that the Greeks were 'completely routed by Mustapha Kemal' (context only). Location: Wavell, Allenby: Soldier and Statesman, pp. 171, 236, 245. Evidence P. See Turkish War of Independence.


## Charles Harington (1940)

*Calls Kemal 'a real soldier' who stopped the Greeks on the Sakarya and 'saw his chance' at Afyon; says Allied arms stocks fed his army.*

> This must have rejoiced the heart of Mustapha Kemal, a real soldier, who held them up quite definitely on the River Sakaria.

Tim Harington Looks Back (John Murray, 1940), p. 107 (running heads '107', '109'); open DLI scan in.ernet.dli.2015.176573. P. 109: 'With the Greeks weakened by their detachment in Eastern Thrace, Mustapha Kemal saw his chance and seized it. He struck a bit of the line near Afium Karahissar'. Elsewhere the Chanak commander says that the arms the Allies stored after 1918 were released to the Turks and 'formed the nucleus of the army which Mustapha Kemal built up', and that Kemal 'sent some very difficult men to Constantinople'. He prints Kemal's and Ismet's letters after the Mudania agreement (c. p. 150). Conflicted rather than admiring: professional respect with the grievance of the Chanak commander. Neutral voice on Chanak. Location: Harington, Tim Harington Looks Back (1940), pp. 107, 109. Evidence P. See Chanak Crisis.


## Dry ends (round 16)

- **Ian Hamilton, *Gallipoli Diary* (1920):** the open Gutenberg text (vols 1-2) never names Kemal. Falkenhayn, *General Headquarters 1914-1916*: no mention of Kemal in the open scan.
- **Aspinall-Oglander, *Military Operations: Gallipoli* vol. 1:** names Mustafa Kemal as the 19th Division's commander ('the future President') but states no view in the open volume; vol. 2 is lending-only.
- **Carl Mühlmann, *Der Kampf um die Dardanellen* (1927):** calls him 'der jetzige Gebieter der Türkei' in passing; narrative, no stated view.
- **T. E. Lawrence, *Seven Pillars* (1922 text, pp. 571-573):** Kemal appears as the Turkish staff officer to whom Feisal's propaganda was aimed ('too keen on the Turkishness of their mission'), who 'begged Feisal not to play into Jemal's hands'; Lawrence reports his plans and states no judgement of him. Not stored.
- **Townshend, *My Campaign in Mesopotamia* (1920), Kress von Kressenstein, Ludendorff, Monash, Keith Murdoch, Mannerheim:** no hit in the archive.org full-text search under author for Kemal. **Montgomery, *A Concise History of Warfare* (1972):** two index entries (pp. 299, 306) for Kemal; lending-only, snippet shows only 'distinguished himself in repelling the Australians'; not stored. **J. F. C. Fuller, *Military History of the Western World* (1956):** narrates Anafarta, no stated view in the snippets. Rommel, Patton, Hammerstein, Frunze: not found. Aralov (Russian): the one search returned nothing under his name.
- **Not reached:** Aralov's *Воспоминания советского дипломата* (Russian print, not on archive.org full text), Frunze's Ankara report (Russian archive), Papoulas and Stratigos (Greek), Monash's letters, Allenby's own despatches.
