# H.C. Armstrong

*Grey Wolf: Mustafa Kemal — An Intimate Study of a Dictator* (1932) was the first major
unauthorised biography. Armstrong had served in the Ottoman theatre in WWI and was posted back to
Turkey after the armistice, watching the rise of the new state up close and in contact with its
leadership. The book gives the career its due while dwelling on the drinking, the gambling and the
women — the "intimate study of a dictator" its subtitle promises.

Turkey banned it immediately, and the ban held: the first Turkish translation appeared only in the
1990s (Mango, *Atatürk*, p. 506). The famous anecdote — that Atatürk had it translated for himself
and said the government had erred, since "that fellow has made too little of our pleasures" — is,
in Mango's words, "another apocryphal story" (p. 506). 🎭 reception data, not fact.

**The book itself (read in full, Oct 2026):** it is mixed, not simply hostile. The racial register
is ugly — power "brought out in him the brute and the beast… the wolf-stock of the central steppes
of Asia" (p. 254) — and the drinking, gambling and women recur throughout; yet the closing chapters
(LXXIV–LXXV) call his rule "a benevolent, educating, guiding dictatorship" and credit his "military
genius." Mango (p. 506) calls it "a sensational mixture of gossip and men's club racism." Polarity:
**conflicted**.

His place in the deck: the **intimate-hostile Western card**, printed the same era as Charles H. Sherrill's admiring embassy memoir — the two gazes side by side in the 1930s bookshop. The
rehabilitated Western gaze came later with Lord Kinross. See [[Historiography of Atatürk]].
