# Hikmat Sulayman

The one Iraqi statesman for whom Mustafa Kemal was an explicit programme. Karol Sorby (*Rocznik
Orientalistyczny* 68/1, 2015) puts it flatly: Hikmat Sulayman "was also interested in reform and in
more rapid economic and social development, but **his model for reform was the paternalistic
authoritarianism of Mustafa Kemal.**" His admiration "had increased after his visit to Turkey in
**1935**, after which he wrote several articles advocating a thoroughgoing secularism and
modernization on Turkish lines" — an attitude that drew him close to General [[Bakr Sidqi]] and the army.

Sorby's second claim ties the two: Sulayman and Sidqi, "intimate friends", "were both inspired by
Kemalist Turkey, which had been able to maintain her independence only through the reorganization
of her army." On that logic Iraq's officers decided that "just as the military regimes in Turkey
and Persia were eliminating foreign control and carrying out reforms, so should the army officers in
Iraq rule their country" (Sorby's paraphrase of the officers' conclusion; the Persian half of it
is Reza Shah Pahlavi). The coup of **29 October 1936** — the first in the modern Arab world —
made Hikmat premier with Sidqi as the strongman behind him.

Caveats. The Sorby paper's sources for the 1935 visit and articles are in its footnotes (Arabic
secondary works; the specific newspaper articles were not read). He had no chance to extend the
programme: Sidqi was assassinated on 11 August 1937 and Hikmat's government soon fell. The
mirror is **Kemal-as-method**: not the nationalism of the Turks but the toolkit — army, state
secularism, speed. The contrasting Iraqi card is the king Faisal I of Iraq, who needed Ankara
as an ally, and Rashid Ali al-Kilani, the pan-Arabist who later led Iraq against Britain.
