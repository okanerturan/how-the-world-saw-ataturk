# Ali Abd al-Raziq

The most famous book ever written in the shadow of Mustafa Kemal's decision — and the one that
barely looks at him. The gaze here is an honest oblique one, and the card is only worth keeping
if that is said plainly: **no sentence of praise or blame for Atatürk has been found in
Abd al-Raziq's text or in the scholarship summarised below.** What exists is a causal and
a political relationship.

**The occasion.** Encyclopedia.com's entry frames *al-Islam wa usul al-hukm* (Cairo, 1925) as
"published only one year after Atatürk's abolition of the caliphate"; the Jadaliyya profile
is cited for calling it a response to the intellectual debates the abolition set off (⚠️ the Jadaliyya page
returned nothing in the 2026-10-29 re-check, so that attribution is unconfirmed; the "occasion" link is
supported only loosely, by Encyclopedia.com's "one year after"). Abd al-Raziq, educated at
al-Azhar (graduated 1911) and at Oxford from 1912, then a sharia-court judge, argued that
neither Quran, hadith nor consensus (*ijma'*) makes the caliphate a religious obligation; that
the Prophet was sent as a preacher, not a ruler; and that government, in his famous formulation,
is left by religion "to our free choice", to be settled by "rational judgement ... the
experience of nations and the rules of politics" (translated in Donohue & Esposito, *Islam in
Transition*, p. 36; excerpted via Hourani's *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*).

**The twist: Cairo, not Ankara, was his target (⚠️ the Guardian's inference, not a statement by Abd al-Raziq).** A caliph had just been made unnecessary in
Turkey, and in Egypt the palace of King Fuad was angling for the title while al-Azhar's
establishment lobbied to relocate the caliphate to Cairo — the 1926 Cairo Caliphate Conference
was the result. The Guardian's 2009 piece notes this setting: a book "denouncing the Caliphate
would have been highly problematic for King Fuad" in 1925. Abd al-Raziq therefore *used* the
Turkish fact: with the Ottoman caliphate gone, he could ask whether any such office was needed.
That is the instrumental reading; whether he personally admired the abolition is not
established by anything here.

**The punishment.** A council of twenty-four leading *'ulama'* of al-Azhar, with the rector at
its head, condemned him in a ruling dated 12 November 1925 (per Prophetic Politics, a
secondary web source): his titles were stripped, he was dismissed from his judgeship and barred
from public office. [[Rashid Rida]], in *al-Manar*, called the book the latest attempt of the
enemies of Islam to divide it from within and welcomed the verdict as a "great manifest
victory". Albert Hourani's summary: a "violent storm". The event that made the question askable is in [[Abolition of the Caliphate]]; for the contrasting jurist-hostility of [[Mustafa Sabri Efendi]], who fled to Egypt.

Why keep him in a census of gazes? Because the Rorschach test has an *indirect* register: a
thinker who sees in an event a licence rather than a person. Later liberals made him "the first
Muslim secularist" (the Guardian's headline); a recent CUNY thesis (2025) rejects that label,
arguing he stayed within Islamic argument. Neither tradition ever turned him into a Kemalist.
